How Do Beginners Use B-Roll to Make Videos Look Professional?

Video Editing · 19 November 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: You use B-roll by filming extra shots that show what your words describe, then you cut to those shots at natural pauses in your main video. Short B-roll clips hide cuts, hold attention, and make the edit look smooth and professional.

You use B-roll to make videos look professional by cutting away from the main shot to footage that shows the story. The cut hides a jump in the main video, and the extra shot gives the viewer something fresh to watch.

Most beginners film only the main shot, and their edits jump each time they cut a pause. This habit makes the video feel rough, because the viewer sees every cut and hears every jump.

You will finish this guide with a simple method for every video you edit. You will plan B-roll shots, place them at natural pauses, and keep each clip short.

Learn What B-Roll Adds to a Video

Learning how to use B-roll in video editing for beginners starts with the purpose, because B-roll serves the story and not the edit alone. B-roll shows the detail your words describe, and it covers the cuts where you trim a pause. The viewer then watches a smooth story, and the edit fades from view.

Cover a cut so the edit feels smooth

Cut to B-roll at the exact point where you trim a pause or a mistake from the main shot, and the viewer never sees the jump. The B-roll fills the gap while the main audio runs on, and the edit sounds like one clean take. A creator in Lahore filmed a cooking video and cut to a close shot of the pan each time she trimmed a pause, and her viewers never noticed the cuts.

Show the detail your words describe

Film a close shot of the object you talk about, such as the hands that knead the dough or the screen that shows the app, and cut to it as you name it. The viewer then sees the proof beside the words, and the lesson lands harder than words alone. You keep the shot simple and bright, because a clear detail beats a fancy angle.

Plan Your B-Roll Shots Before You Film

Good B-roll starts before you press record, because a short plan beats a random pile of clips. You list the shots you need for each scene, and you film them while the light and the location still suit you. The list then guides the whole shoot, and you return home with every shot you need. This is the second core move in how to use B-roll in video editing for beginners, because a plan saves a second trip.

Write a short shot list for each scene

Write three or four shots for each scene, such as a wide shot of the room, a close shot of the hands, and a detail shot of the tool. The list keeps your filming tight, and it stops you from filming the same angle twice. A beginner who filmed a bike repair video wrote his list the night before, and he finished the shoot in one hour with every shot in hand.

Film extra shots while you have the location

Film a few extra shots of the place while you stand there, such as the street outside, the shelf of tools, or the steam that rises from the pan. The extra shots cost minutes now, and they save you a return trip later. You also film each shot for a few seconds longer than you think you need, because a long clip gives you room to trim in the edit.

Place B-Roll Clips With Care in the Edit

Placement decides whether B-roll feels natural or forced, so you cut to it at a pause in the story. You watch the main shot and mark the points where the speaker breathes or the topic shifts, and you place a B-roll clip on each mark. The clip then feels like a natural glance away, and the viewer follows it without a jolt. This care gives you the third core move in how to use B-roll in video editing for beginners, because a well placed clip vanishes into the story.

Cut to B-roll at a natural pause

Place the clip where the speaker finishes a thought, and return to the main shot before the next thought starts. The pause hides the cut, and the viewer reads the change as a visual breath. A vlogger in Karachi cut to street shots only at the end of each thought, and her videos gained a calm rhythm her viewers praised.

Keep each clip short enough to hold attention

Hold most B-roll clips for two to four seconds, and cut back before the shot grows dull. A short clip adds flavour without stealing the scene, and it keeps the pace brisk from start to finish. You use a longer clip only when the shot itself tells a story, such as a slow pour or a craft in progress. Find Free B-Roll When You Cannot Film It

You cannot always film the shot you need, so free stock libraries fill the gap with care. You search a free library for the scene you need, and you download only clips that allow free use in your videos. This habit forms the last core move in how to use B-roll in video editing for beginners, because stock saves a shoot you cannot make.

Use stock libraries that allow free use

Pick a known free library, such as Pexels or Pixabay, and search for the exact scene you need. The libraries offer clips you can use without paying, and each clip shows its licence beside the download button. You match the stock to your own footage in light and colour, because a mismatched clip stands out at once.

Check the licence before you publish

Read the licence on each stock clip before you use it, and confirm that it allows use in videos you publish. Most free libraries allow this use, but a few clips ask for credit to the creator, so you add the credit where the platform asks for it. The check takes a minute, and it protects your video from a takedown later. Frequently Asked Questions

What is B-roll in video editing?

B-roll is extra footage that shows what your words describe, such as close shots of hands, places, or objects. Editors cut to B-roll to cover a cut in the main shot, and the viewer sees a smooth story instead of a jump. Editors call the main footage A-roll, and B-roll supports it.

How long should B-roll clips be?

Most B-roll clips work well at two to four seconds, because a short clip adds flavour without stealing the scene. You hold a clip longer only when the shot itself tells a story, such as a slow pour. You cut back to the main shot before the B-roll grows dull.

Where can I find free B-roll footage?

Free libraries such as Pexels and Pixabay offer B-roll clips you can download and use without paying. You search for the exact scene you need, and you check the licence beside each clip. You match the stock to your own footage in light and colour, so it blends in.

Do I need B-roll in every video?

You need B-roll in most videos that cut pauses or jump between ideas, because it hides the cuts. A single uncut take needs no B-roll, because nothing breaks the flow. You add B-roll where the edit needs cover, and you leave it out where the shot already works.

How do I film good B-roll with a phone?

You film B-roll with a phone by holding it steady, filming in good light, and moving slowly around the subject. You film each shot a few seconds longer than you need, because a long clip gives you room to trim. You also film from two or three angles, so the edit has choice.

What is the difference between B-roll and A-roll?

A-roll is the main footage that carries the story, such as a person who talks to the camera. B-roll is the extra footage that supports it, such as close shots of the hands or the place. Editors cut from A-roll to B-roll and back, and the two together make a smooth video.

Can B-roll hide mistakes in my main footage?

B-roll can hide a cut where you trim a pause or a slip, because the viewer watches the new shot while the audio runs on. It cannot fix bad audio or dark footage, because the main shot still carries the sound. You fix the sound and the light first, and you use B-roll to smooth the picture.

How much B-roll should I film for one video?

You film three or four B-roll shots for each scene, because that range gives the edit choice without a long shoot. You also film a few extra shots of the place while you stand there, because extras save a return trip. A short list before the shoot keeps the filming tight and fast.
