How Do Freelancers Raise Their Rates With Existing Clients?

Freelancing · 18 November 2026 · 8 min read

Short answer: You raise your rates with existing clients by naming the new rate early, giving thirty days of notice, and tying the rise to a clear improvement in your work. You send one short message, and you keep the tone warm. This guide shows you how freelancers raise their rates with existing clients without losing the work.

Most freelancers wait too long, then raise the rate in a sudden jump with no warning. The client feels shocked, and a good working relationship turns sour in one message.

You finish with a rate rise your clients accept, a short message you can reuse, and the confidence to raise your rate again.

Pick the Right Time and the Right Size for the Rise

A rate rise lands well when the timing feels fair, so raise the rate after a clear improvement or when your calendar stays full. You link the rise to a skill you gained or a result you delivered, and the client sees the exchange as fair. A small rise each year feels normal, while a sudden double feels like a shock. You plan the rise in advance, and you tell the client first.

Raise the rate after a clear win

Pick a recent win, such as a project that earned the client more sales, and name it in your message. A writer who learned SEO raised her rate after her first ranked article, and her clients paid the new rate because the work earned more traffic. This link between the rise and the win gives the client a reason to stay. Raise the rate within a week of the win, while the result still feels fresh.

Keep each rise small and steady

A rise of ten to twenty percent feels fair to most clients, while a rise of fifty percent shocks even a happy client. You test a small rise first, and you watch how the client responds before you plan the next one. A designer who raised her rate by a small step kept all three of her regular clients. Small rises add up, and they protect the relationship that earns you steady work.

Write the Message That Carries the New Rate

The message carries the whole rise, so you should write it short, warm, and clear. You name the new rate, the date it starts, and one line about the improvement behind it. A long apology weakens the message, because it tells the client that you doubt your own price. You keep the message short, and this short message sits at the heart of how freelancers raise their rates with existing clients.

Name the rate, the date, and one reason

Open with thanks for the work you shared, then name the new rate and the date it starts. A freelancer who wrote this message kept it to three lines, and his client accepted the rise in one reply. You close by asking the client to confirm the new rate for the next project, so the client can answer with one word. This short shape respects the client, and it shows that you value your own work.

Send the message thirty days before the change

Give the client thirty days of notice, so the client can plan the budget and finish current work at the old rate. A client who learns the new rate with the next invoice feels trapped, and the trust you built over months cracks in one bill. Send the message by email, and follow it with a short call when the client stays silent for a week. Handle the Reply Without Cutting Your Price

A client may push back, and your answer decides whether the rise holds or falls. You listen to the real reason behind the reply, and you separate a price objection from a timing problem. A client who asks for time may accept the rise next month, while a client who asks for a discount tests your floor. You hold the rate steady, and you offer a smaller scope instead of a lower price.

Offer a smaller scope instead of a discount

When a client says the budget is tight, you offer fewer deliverables at your new rate instead of cutting the price. A designer who offered three pages instead of five kept her new rate, and the client still received a site that worked. This trade protects your floor, and it still serves the client. You state the smaller scope clearly, so the client knows exactly what the new price buys.

Know when to let a client go

A client who refuses every rise for a year costs you more than the work pays, so part with warmth when the gap stays wide. You thank the client for the work you shared, and you name a final date for the old rate. A freelancer who let one low paying client go freed ten hours a month. Keep the New Rate After the First Yes

A rise only works when the next invoice carries the new rate, so you should update your price list the day the client accepts. You note the new rate in your records, and you quote it on the next proposal without hesitation. A freelancer who forgets the rise bills the old rate for months, and the client never asks for the higher number. You guard the new rate by writing it down at once.

Quote the new rate on the next proposal

Send the new rate in your next proposal without apology and without a discount. A freelancer who raised his rate by a small step won two of three proposals, and the higher fee covered the one he lost. You learn the true value of the rate when real clients answer it. Review your rates every six months

Open your records twice a year, and compare your rate with your costs, your skills, and your calendar. A full calendar for weeks tells you that the market accepts a higher rate, while a quiet calendar tells you to sharpen the offer first. You raise the rate in small steps after each review, and you tell current clients early each time. This habit turns rate rises into a routine, and the routine protects your income as your skills grow.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do freelancers raise their rates with existing clients without losing them?

How much should I raise my rates?

You should raise your rate by a small step, such as ten to twenty percent, and watch how clients respond. A small rise feels fair, while a sudden jump shocks even a happy client. Test the small rise first, and plan the next one only after the first one holds.

How much notice should I give before a rate rise?

You should give thirty days of notice, so the client can plan the budget and finish current work at the old rate. A client who learns the new rate with the next invoice feels trapped. Send the message by email, and follow it with a short call when the client stays silent.

What if a client refuses the new rate?

You should ask what budget the client holds, and offer a smaller scope at your new rate instead of cutting the price. A smaller scope protects your floor, and it still serves the client. If the budget stays far below your floor, you should part with warmth and move on.

Should I raise rates for all clients at once?

You can raise rates for all clients at once when the rise is small and the notice is clear. You should still send each client a personal message, because a personal note feels more respectful than a bulk notice. Stagger the rises when the rise is large, so you can watch each reply.

How often should freelancers raise their rates?

You should review your rates twice a year, and raise them after a clear improvement or when your calendar stays full. Small rises each year feel normal to clients. Tie each rise to a skill you gained or a result you delivered.

Do I need a reason to raise my rates?

Yes, you should name one clear reason, such as a new skill or a strong result for the client. A reason turns the rise into a fair exchange, and it gives the client a reason to stay. Avoid long excuses, because a clear reason earns more respect than a page of apology.

What should I do if I lose a client over a rate rise?

You should thank the client, close the work cleanly, and use the freed hours to find a client who pays the new rate. One lost client hurts less than a year of work below your floor. Keep the door open, and let the client return when the budget allows.
